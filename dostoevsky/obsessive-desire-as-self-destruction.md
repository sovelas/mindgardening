Rogojin's account of his first encounter with [[nastasia-philipovnas-transformation|Nastasia Philipovna]]. He sees her step into a carriage and is "all of a blaze at once." He embezzles his father's money for diamond earrings, stands mute and shaking in her drawing room, contemplates the Neva, collapses with fever in Pskoff. 

Desire not something he chooses but something he undergoes. It's bodily, irrational, and potentially fatal. 

[[myshkin-as-holy-fool|Myshkin]]'s later prediction, "marry her tomorrow and murder her in a week," sees this passion as carrying destruction under it.

[[Dostoevsky]] is in a context that connects [[Romanticism]]'s celebration of overwhelming feeling with a clinical awareness of its costs. Rogojin's desire shares territory with [[Stendhal]]'s [[crystallization-in-love|crystallization]] (his imagination transforms a the real person into an idealized object) but pushes further into pathology. 

Also, Rogojin never expresses concern for Nastasia's wellbeing; his passion manifests entirely as a desire for access, through gifts, through money, through proximity. 

[[love-as-possession-in-the-idiot]]: is the experience of overwhelming romantic intensity just self-centered?
