[[the-marie-parable|Marie]] "thought it quite right and fitting" that she was treated as the lowest creature. She accepted abuse from her dying mother "without a word," nursed her tormentor faithfully, and never protested the pastor's public humiliation. 

Even when the [[children-as-moral-compass-in-dostoevsky|children]]'s love transformed her final weeks, she accepted their kindness "as a sort of symbol of pardon for her offence, though she never ceased to consider herself a dreadful sinner." 

Social punishment produces not just external suffering but internal collapse. 
The shame is no longer imposed from outside only.
The victim comes to believe in her own unworthiness. 

[[nastasia-philipovnas-transformation|Nastasia Philipovna]], also oscillates between fierce defiance and self-loathing, as I myself often do. Between [[weaponized-self-destruction|weaponized self-destruction]] and the desperate wish to be told she is "good." 

Similar to what [[trauma psychology]]/[[complex PTSD]] later describe: prolonged victimization produces a characteristic reshaping of identity around the experience of worthlessness. [[Erving Goffman]]'s concept of the [[spoiled identity]] in which stigma becomes the master status through which a person interprets all experience also maps onto Marie. 

[[Myshkin-as-holy-fool|Myshkin]] does not argue Marie out of her shame or try to convince her she is innocent. He just surrounds her with kindness that does not require her to be anything other than what she is. 
Still open if this is sufficient to heal it or merely to makes it bearable.
