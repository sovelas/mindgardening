In Chapter V of [[the-idiot|The Idiot]], [[myshkin-as-holy-fool|Prince Myshkin]] describes his Swiss convalescence as a period of increasing happiness. Not caused by events. Caused by attention. 

"Every day became dearer and more precious to me, and the longer I stayed, the dearer became the time to me." 

A donkey braying in the Basel market breaks his post-seizure torpor. A waterfall, a mountain, a ruined castle. None of it particularly extraordinary, yet wonderful because he is present to them. 

"You can be happy? Then how can you say you did not learn to see? I should think you could teach us to see!" (Aglaya)

The chapter frames happiness as a skill of perception, something closer to a practice than a feeling.

Similar to [[David Foster Wallace]]'s [[this-is-water-overview|"This Is Water"]] argument that the real value of education is the choice of what to pay attention to, and with [[Simone Weil]]'s concept of [[attention]] as the rarest and purest form of generosity. 
It also connects to [[contemplative traditions]] like [[Zen Buddhism]], [[Christian mysticism]], the [[Stoic]] practice of [[prosoche]] (attention). 

But in The Idiot there's a specific clinical context: Myshkin's heightened perception is partly a product of epilepsy and illness.
A sort of spiritual attainment by (neurological) accident.
