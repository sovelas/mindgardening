[[humanism|Humanism]]  treats *the human as such* as a free-standing object of inquiry, against the [[scholastic-merger|scholastic merger]] of philosophy and theology. Doing so it sets up a trap. The universal "human" abstracts away from the particular, and your [[rootedness-not-introspection-against-shaped-preference|rooted, particular life]] is gets hollowed out. So it seems all the time you have to pick a side to you stand on, the universal or the particular?

I do not believe you can't have both. You can conceptualize the universal human and have that rooted in your own experience; one strengthens the other. That I, for example, can from my own specific suffering infer better what general human suffering is, just makes both my own experience and my conceptualization of human suffering richer.

And in any case, "humans in general" IS NOT a real thing. It's a concept, a tool that helps us project what might be real. Just because it's not real doesn't mean it can't generate real consequences.

While the particular can strengthen or give body to the universal, it can also be true that more particulars can take a better picture of the universal, or that I can know that my particular experience as well as my generalization can be wrong, and maybe that's the best tool I've got.

**Iterating through myself/other, particular/universal, inside/outside, is/ought seems to me to generate the picture of the world that I can most trust and be in peace with.**

The position sidesteps both the [[philosophical-perspectivism|perspectivist]] dead end (every view is local, so no view) and the false-neutrality move [[the-aesthetic-attitude-and-scientific-inquiry|Beauvoir critiques]] in scientific objectivity (a view from nowhere). Neither pole is firm ground. The oscillation between them is.
