The step back of not only trying to study the scientific methods but understanding *where they come from* shows how much of our "default settings" are hidden. [[Husserl]]'s move in [[the-crisis-of-european-sciences|*Crisis*]] isn't just to point at the [[bracket-drifts-into-metaphysics|bracket-drift]] in modern science; it's to trace it historically. Through [[Galileo]], [[Descartes]], the long arc of post-medieval thought... until you can see when the choice was made and what got chosen against.

You can see it well when you look back at the history of thought and notice "ah, I guess things that now seem obvious to me were not so obvious back then"; and that there seems to be a whole multi-era trajectory you have to look into to be able to see it, trace it back to where it came from, and notice how it could and maybe should start moving in a different direction.

That seems to me what Husserl is trying to say. The diagnosis isn't available from inside the [[natural-attitude|natural attitude]]; you have to *historicize* the natural attitude to see it as an attitude at all. What looks like "just how things are" is the deposited residue of a chain of choices, most of them made by people who are now dead and whose reasons we no longer remember.

This is structurally the same gesture as [[situated-perception|situating perception]], except the situating is *temporal* rather than spatial. You don't escape your defaults by pretending to a [[the-view-from-nowhere-as-position|view from nowhere]]; you escape them (partially) by walking back through the trajectory that produced them. Genealogy as un-bracketing.

The move pairs with [[particular<->universal|the iteration]] from the prior note: myself/other, particular/universal, inside/outside, is/ought — and now also *now/then*. The trustworthy picture comes from oscillating across all of these, including across time.
