If every human has an equal claim on our concern, what's the political *unit* that delivers it? [[Martha Nussbaum|Nussbaum]]'s answer is the surprise of the book — surprising because her 1994 essay "Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism" leaned the other way.

She lands on the **nation-state**. Following [[Hugo Grotius]], she treats it as the largest unit that can actually be an effective home of human autonomy and stay accountable to people's voices. Cosmopolitan *ends*, liberal-nationalist *means*.

Critics pounce (e.g. the LSE review): [[Cicero]] privileged the Republic, and Grotius *founded* the philosophical defence of the nation-state — so is this a *cosmopolitan* tradition at all, or nationalism in cosmopolitan dress? And a nation-state offers, at best, the *possibility* of dignity, never a guarantee.

> [my take — is the nation-state the right "rooted home" for the universal, or a retreat dressed up as realism?]

For me this is the same refusal I make in [[particular<->universal]]: the universal human, conceived only in the abstract, hollows out the particular life — so maybe the universal can only be reached *through* a rooted, particular home, not around it.

And the garden's own motto is the tempting opposite error: Voltaire's "we must cultivate our garden" ([[freedom-as-commodity|Candide]]) — tending your own plot and forgetting the world. The live question is the narrow path between Candide's retreat and pure cosmopolitan abstraction.
