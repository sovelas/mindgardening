[[Martha Nussbaum]]'s *The Cosmopolitan Tradition* (subtitle: *A Noble but Flawed Ideal*) is four essays tracing one lineage: the Cynic–Stoic idea of the *kosmou politēs*, the world-citizen. [[Diogenes]] says "I am a citizen of the world", and the [[the Stoics|Stoics]] turn it into a claim about equal worth — every human has dignity independent of city, class, gender or status.

That's the **noble** part, and Nussbaum thinks it's the root of our whole idea of equal human dignity.

The **flaw** is baked into the same move. The tradition grounds dignity in our *rational and moral capacity*, and then prizes a radical self-sufficiency: virtue alone is good, and the sage's flourishing is untouched by misfortune, poverty, even slavery. So health, food, political freedom, family — the external goods a real human needs — get filed as morally indifferent.

She follows the thread through [[Cicero]], [[Hugo Grotius]] and [[Adam Smith]], and argues it culminates (once corrected) in her own [[the capabilities approach]].

Two flaws are worth their own beds: the [[the-bifurcation-of-duties|split between duties of justice and duties of aid]], and the way [[dignity-needs-external-goods|dignity gets hollowed out by self-sufficiency]]. Her controversial landing — cosmopolitan goals run *through* the nation-state — is in [[cosmopolitan-through-the-local]].

Underneath it all is the same knot I keep pulling at in [[iterating-particular-and-universal-as-method]]: how the universal "human" relates to a rooted, particular life.
