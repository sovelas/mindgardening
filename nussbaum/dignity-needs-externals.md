The [[the Stoics|Stoic]] move that *grounds* equal dignity is also the one that *guts* it.

Ground dignity in our rational and moral capacity — not in health, wealth or luck — and you get a beautiful equality: the enslaved person and the emperor have exactly the same worth. But push it to the Stoic limit and external goods become mere "indifferents": if the sage's flourishing survives poverty, illness, even slavery, then nobody's dignity actually *needs* anything.

[[Martha Nussbaum|Nussbaum]]'s charge is that this curdles dignity into a *macho* ideal — self-command, indifference to misfortune — which she finds still lingering in [[Adam Smith]]. And it has an ugly downstream: if dignity needs nothing external, the destitute have no material claim on us, and you are a short step from blaming them for not rising above their conditions.

Her correction: dignity is the dignity of a *needy, embodied animal*. A capacity is worth nothing unless the material and social conditions let you actually exercise it — which is the whole point of [[the capabilities approach]].

This sits very close to [[affliction|Weil on affliction]]: real deprivation can crush the soul, you cannot simply will your way above it, and the ethical demand is to *attend to the needy person*, not to admire the self-sufficient one.
