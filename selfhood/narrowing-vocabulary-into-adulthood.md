Becoming an adult is, effectively, narrowing your vocabulary again after an exhausting effort to broaden it. Childhood and school go into widening the set of words, concepts and ways of seeing — and then adulthood quietly prunes it back, until you can no longer parse the openness of a child's speech: it simply doesn't fit the narrow set of concepts you've kept.

I see it at the edges. My nephews ask "could we take the train to Paris?" and the adult reflex is to answer with all the reasons not to. [[nuance-versus-the-straight-answer|At work I'm coached the opposite way]] — strip the nuance, give the clean answer people can follow. Both are the same pruning: fewer branches, faster traffic.

I don't want to narrow. The flattening is a real loss — you stop being able to *hear* possibilities that don't survive translation into your existing concepts. But I don't want to disintegrate either, to keep every branch and never manage to say anything. The honest task sits between the two: a vocabulary wide enough to keep hearing, disciplined enough to still mean something. See also [[individuality-within-culture]].
