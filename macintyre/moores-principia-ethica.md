[[G.E. Moore]]'s *Principia Ethica* (1903) makes three claims. 

First, "good" names a simple, indefinable, non-natural property (not just meaning something is "pleasant" or "conducive to survival") knowable only by [[intuition|moral intuition]]. 

Second, an action is right if it produces more good than any available alternative, making Moore a [[utilitarianism|utilitarian]]. 

Third, the greatest goods are personal affections (friendship, love, intimate connection) and aesthetic enjoyments, this is declared "the ultimate and fundamental truth of Moral Philosophy." 

MacIntyre notes these claims are logically independent: one could hold any without the others. He also notes that the first is plainly false, the others highly controversial, and the arguments often defective. Yet figures like [[John Maynard Keynes]], [[Lytton Strachey]], and [[Virginia Woolf]] treated the book as a revolution.
