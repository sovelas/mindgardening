[[MacIntyre]] tries to rescue [[emotivism-as-theory-of-meaning|emotivism]] by recasting it. Even if he believes the *meaning* of "This is good" is not only equivalent to "I approve of this; do so as well," it might still be the case that in *using* such sentences, speakers are in fact doing nothing other than expressing feelings and attempting to manipulate others' attitudes. 

If this is true, meaning and use would be discrepant. Moral language still wears the costume of objectivity, even when it is functioning as preference and pressure. The speaker might sincerely believe they are appealing to impersonal criteria while all they are actually doing is expressing preference in a manipulative way. 

This reinterpretation transforms emotivism from a universal thesis about moral language into an empirical, historically specific claim: in cultures that have lost genuine access to objective moral standards, moral language persists in form but functions emotivistically in practice. 

It becomes, as he puts it, "a cogent theory of use rather than a false theory of meaning," connected to a specific stage in moral development (or decline).
