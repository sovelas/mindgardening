The essay argues that [[jigsaw-as-moral-villain|Jigsaw]] is best understood not as a [[nihilism|nihilist]] or a liberal reformer, but as a strict patriarch. The tyrannical father figure who believes suffering is love, that harsh discipline saves souls, that obedience and gratitude can be beaten into people. His traps are tests of will, but they are also tests of submission. He is always giving instructions: follow the rules, make your choice, learn your lesson. Free will matters to him, but only within the confines of an ordeal he has already designed.

The rhetoric of care, rehabilitation, and spiritual growth disguises domination. He insists his project is altruistic (awakening rather than punishment, rebirth rather than retribution) yet his methods are plainly sadistic. The supposedly benevolent project of [[justice-retribution-and-rehabilitation|rehabilitation]] justifies even more brutal violence than straightforward revenge would.

It wraps cruelty in the language of love.

Jigsaw does not contradict retributive justice. He is the figure who believes punishment *is* rehabilitation, that pain teaches, and that people deserve whatever happens to them if they fail his test.
