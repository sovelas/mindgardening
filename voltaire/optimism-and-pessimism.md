The novel seems to argue that optimism and pessimism are not opposites but twins with the same flaw.

Pangloss claims all is for the best; 
Martin claims a malignant being governs the world. 
Candide and Martin debate for fifteen days at sea and reach no conclusion. 

Voltaire positions both frameworks as [[unfalsifiability|unfalsifiable]] and therefore useless: optimism ignores evidence of evil, pessimism ignores evidence of good, and neither helps anyone do anything.

Martin has a different predictive power. He wins every wager about human unhappiness, but no capacity for action or consolation. 

Pangloss's doctrine at least permits activity (however misguided). Martin's worldview counsels only observation and confirmed expectation. 

His "I have lived" sounds like authority but may also be surrender. 

Both men arrive at the garden: Pangloss still asserting what he no longer believes, Martin stopping dispute out of exhaustion rather than insight.

The Dervish slams his door on Pangloss's theological question. The Turkish farmer offers no theory. The garden replaces philosophical debate with manual activity, not because the questions are unimportant but because the debating mode itself is sterile.

This connects to [[Camus]]'s parallel rejection of both [[philosophical-suicide|philosophical suicide]] (systems that deny the absurd) and actual nihilism, proposing [[revolt-against-the-absurd|revolt]] as a third path. It also resonates with [[pragmatism]] in the [[William James]] tradition: the value of a belief measured by its consequences for action rather than its correspondence to reality.
