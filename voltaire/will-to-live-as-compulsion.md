An old woman meditates on why humans "detest existence and yet cling to one's existence". She has observed countless people who curse their lives yet almost none who end them: only "three negroes, four Englishmen, and a German professor." Her conclusion is that the attachment to life is not courage, hope. It is an irrational compulsion, a "ridiculous foible". [[Something that operates below the level of deliberation]].

Voltaire leaves the question genuinely open (?) 
Is the will to live a form of wisdom deeper than any philosophy? 
Or is it a trap that keeps us "caressing the serpent which devours us"? 

Related to [[Schopenhauer]]'s concept of the [[Will]] as a blind force beneath consciousness, or [[Camus]]'s opening question in *The Myth of Sisyphus*: whether life is worth living is the only truly serious philosophical question. 
But where Camus treats the question as one demanding a deliberate answer (revolt, not suicide), Voltaire's old woman suggests the answer has already been given (?)
The body tells us before the question is even posed.

The observation also challenges both Pangloss and Martin. 
Optimism claims life is good; pessimism claims it is terrible. The old woman, I guess, would say who cares?: both persist regardless, and no system can really explain exactly why.
