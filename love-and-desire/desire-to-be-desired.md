[[Jacques Lacan|Lacan]] observes that for many people, the primary aim in love is not to love but to *be desired*. What is sought is an ideal version of oneself reflected, accepted, and affirmed in the eyes of someone admirable. In this configuration, the beloved is less of an end in themselves and more of a mirror in which a flattering self-image can appear.

This overlaps with [[stendhal-on-love|Stendhal's]] concept of [[vain-love]]: the tendency to pursue affection not for the other person's qualities but for the validation their affection would provide. The two thinkers seem to agree that romantic desire often has a hidden reflexive structure. It loops back toward the self even when it appears to be directed outward.

This does not mean love is narcissistic. [[Stendhal on Love|Stendhal]] does not condemn vain love; he simply identifies it as one of the things people call love. 

**If we truly love someone, their love for us will almost inevitably affect the image we have of ourselves.** 

The important point is that genuine admiration and ego gratification can coexist. We can experience both simultaneously, which is part of what makes the "crush" so intense and so confusing.
