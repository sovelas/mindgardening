 [[acceptance|Acceptance]]: the approach found in [[Stoicism]], many [[Buddhism|Eastern traditions]], and various philosophies of equanimity. The promise is that if we learn to accept what is ultimately out of our control, those painful realities will lose their power over us. [[emil-cioran|Cioran]] was deeply skeptical of it on two fronts.

First, he doubted that anyone actually achieves the radical acceptance they claim. 
We accept death but not the hour of our death, as he put it. 
It is easy to accept an abstract future meteor strike; it is another thing entirely when fire is raining on everything you love, as West put it. 

The [[gap between intellectual acceptance and lived experience in the moment of crisis]] made Cioran suspect that most claims of acceptance were just another [[sophisticated rationalization designed to defuse discomfort]].

Second, even granting full acceptance, Cioran argued it is not enough to sustain a human life. In a meaningless universe, getting off the couch and doing anything beyond eating and sleeping requires moral, aesthetic, religious, social, etc reasons. All of which involve the kinds of intellectually dishonest leaps that acceptance was supposed to transcend. 

Similar to his broader argument in [[nothingness-as-ciorans-central-premise|nothingness]]: you cannot live from acceptance alone because life demands illusions to function.
