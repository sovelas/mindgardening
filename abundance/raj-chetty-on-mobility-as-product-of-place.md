[[Raj Chetty]] (economist) and his research team have documented that upward [[economic mobility]] is in structural decline in the United States. A child born into an American household in 1940 had a 92 percent chance of earning more than their parents. A child born in the 1980s (just 40 years later) has just a 50 percent chance.

Chetty found that mobility is a product of place. 
A child born poor in San Jose has three times the likelihood of ending up wealthy as a child born poor in Charlotte. Among children who moved from economically stagnant zip codes to richer neighborhoods, the likelihood of better outcomes improved steadily with every extra year spent in the new city, with the earliest movers faring best. 
This means that access to dynamic cities during childhood has compounding, durable effects on life outcomes.

Chetty's team also found that children who moved to high-innovation areas when young were much more likely to patent inventions as adults and the effect was specific to the local specialty. 
(Innovation, like virtue in [[Alasdair MacIntyre]]'s framework, is transmitted through immersion in a [[shared-practices-as-teleology|community of practice]].)
