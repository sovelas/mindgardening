[[Albert Camus]] sees the entire tradition of philosophical system-building as rooted in the avoidance of personal suffering [[nietzsche-suffering-is-not-something-to-be-avoided-just-is]]. Philosophers and religious thinkers encounter [[the-absurd-in-camus|the absurd]] and then create systems of theoretical abstractions or grand unifying narratives to escape that discomfort. Oh, make it make sense! They treat the absurd as a [[seeing-things-as-puzzles-to-be-solved|problem to be solved]] rather than a condition to be inhabited.

The result, Camus argues, is transparently self-serving and ultimately dangerous. These systems promise neat moral justifications that claim to apply to every scenario, but they failed catastrophically when tested against [[fascism]]. [[utilitarianism|Utilitarian]] arguments were too weak to oppose [[Adolf Hitler]]. Fatalism rendered people passive, waiting for the end of history. [[Realism]] bred [[nihilism]], leaving people incapable of action. The philosophers who were supposedly providing moral leadership had created beautiful abstractions and then proved helpless when it mattered most.

The -ism crumbles when reality weathers it. 

So what is the solution? The question itself reveals the problem. Asking for an alternative *system* is asking him to play the same game! To pretend that philosophy can provide justifications for human behavior. That pretense, Camus insists, is more dangerous than having no counter-system at all, because it keeps people believing such justifications are possible. What he wants instead is a [[revolt-against-the-absurd|new way of being]] that doesn't require grounding in abstractions.
