Rush-hour train, a cabin of ten adults and one little girl. As far as I can tell, only two people are actually *there*: the girl, balancing on her feet over and over, watching the window go by — and me, watching her, delighted.

Everyone else is technically present and entirely elsewhere. The child isn't *doing* anything; she is just attending, fully, to a small thing, and that alone makes her the most alive person in the carriage. And the only way I get to share it is by attending to her in turn.

It's a tiny instance of the thing [[happiness-as-attention|Myshkin]] kept insisting on — that the richness isn't in the events but in the seeing — and of [[attention-as-sacrament|attention as the rarest and purest form of generosity]]. The adults all have somewhere to be. Presence is the thing we trade away first, and a child hasn't learned to trade it yet.
