In their book *Homelessness Is a Housing Problem*, [[Gregg Colburn]] and [[Clayton Page Aldern]] systematically test the standard explanations for why [[homelessness]] varies across American cities. Their idea is to identify whether a commonly cited driver of individual homelessness actually predicts *where* homelessness is prevalent.

Higher [[poverty]] rates? No. Cities with high poverty (Detroit, Miami, Dallas, Cincinnati, Philadelphia) have low rates of homelessness. It is richer cities with low overall poverty that see more homelessness. 

Higher [[unemployment]]? The same inversion is true: homelessness is low where unemployment is high and high where unemployment is low. 

[[Mental illness]]? Homelessness is slightly *less* common in states with the highest rates of serious mental illness. 

[[Drug use]]? A slightly positive relationship, but drug use explains only about 5 percent of the variance between places.

What's left that does predict homelessness? The availability and cost of housing. 

When they test these variables, their previously scattered data points coalesce into clear lines: as rent rises, homelessness rises; as vacancy rates plummet, homelessness rises. 

[[California]] has 12 percent of the nation's population but 30 percent of its homeless population and 50 percent of its unsheltered homeless. The cause is not liberal drug policy or generous social services. It is a [[housing supply]] crisis.
